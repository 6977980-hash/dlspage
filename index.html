Rain in a Club T20: An Organiser's Checklist for Fair Targets

By Ch Alee · Club cricketer and tournament organiser · October 2026

I played club cricket for about twenty years and now run local tournaments. The arguments I remember best did not start over a run-out or an lbw. They started when rain stopped play and two captains each had their own idea of what the new target should be.

In an international match the umpires and the match referee have software and a scorer who has done this many times. In a weekend club T20 you usually have a scorebook, a phone and a ground that is getting wetter. This page is the checklist I now give to every umpire and scorer before a tournament starts, so the target is settled by the numbers and not by whoever shouts loudest.

Before the first ball: agree on three things

  1. Which rain rule you use. Write "DLS, Standard Edition" in the tournament rules. Most club disputes happen because nobody agreed on a method beforehand, and someone suggests "just use the run rate" halfway through.
  2. The minimum overs for a result. For T20 cricket the usual figure is 5 overs per side; many knockout games use 10. Decide it before the tournament, not during the rain delay.
  3. Who announces the target. One person, usually the scorer or the senior umpire, works it out and tells both captains at the same time.

When the rain arrives: write down four numbers

A revised target can only be as good as the information behind it. The moment players leave the field, the scorer should note:

  1. The score when play stopped (runs and wickets).
  2. The exact overs bowled, in overs and balls, such as 8.3.
  3. The number of overs lost once play resumes (the umpires decide this).
  4. The first innings total and how many overs Team 1 had, if the stoppage is in the chase.

Wickets matter as much as runs. A side at 60 for 1 and a side at 60 for 6 are in very different positions, and that is the whole idea behind DLS: it measures the batting resources (overs left and wickets in hand) that each side has lost, not just the run rate.

Why "average run rate" is unfair

The oldest club habit is to scale the target by overs: if Team 1 made 160 in 20 overs, a 12-over chase needs 8 runs an over, so 96 to tie and 97 to win. It looks simple, but it ignores the fact that the chasing side keeps all ten wickets for a much shorter innings. Twelve overs with ten wickets in hand let a batting side attack from the first ball.

Ten extra runs is a big gap in a 12-over game, and it is the reason DLS replaced the run rate method in professional cricket. Under DLS, a 20-over innings with 10 wickets is 56.6% of a full 50-over innings, and a 12-over innings with 10 wickets is 37.6%. Team 2 has fewer resources, so its target goes down, but not as far as a simple overs ratio suggests.

Rain in the middle of the chase

Now say the chase begins with the full 20 overs, and rain stops play at 8 overs with Team 2 on 60 for 2.

  • If 5 overs are lost and the chase becomes 15 overs, the DLS target is 123. The par score shows who is ahead at any moment of the rest of the chase.
  • If no more play is possible, the 5-over minimum has been reached, so the match has a result. The DLS par score at 8 overs with 2 wickets down is 58. Team 2 has 60, so Team 2 wins by 2 runs.

Notice the second case. With just one more wicket down, at 60 for 3, the par would be 63 and Team 2 would have lost by 3 runs with the same score. This is the conversation an organiser should be able to settle in one minute, with the numbers on a phone screen.

How a rain-cut game counts in the points table

In a league, the rain match also changes net run rate, and this is where many organisers make a second mistake.

  • For a match decided by DLS, each side's runs and overs in NRR come from the revised match, not the original 20 overs. If the chase was cut to 15 overs, the chasing side's innings counts as 15 overs.
  • If a side is bowled out, its innings counts as its full quota of overs for that match (the reduced quota in a rain-cut game), not the overs it actually faced.
  • A match abandoned without a result usually gives each side one point and does not count in NRR at all.

Once the table has more than a few teams, I stop doing this by hand and enter each result in a net run rate calculator so that every team's figure comes from the same rules.

The one-page version for your umpires

  1. Agree the method (DLS) and the minimum overs before the tournament.
  2. At every stoppage, record runs, wickets, overs and balls bowled.
  3. Get the overs lost from the umpires, then work out the target or par once.
  4. Tell both captains at the same time, and show them the working.
  5. After the match, enter the revised overs, not the original ones, in the NRR table.

Rain will always be frustrating, but it does not have to end in an argument. A clear rule, four written numbers and a calculator everyone trusts are usually enough.
